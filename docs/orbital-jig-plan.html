Orbital scanning jig — drives, mounts, and the app

a study with parts, not a wish list the capture animation →

The jig as printed turns by hand and changes elevation by loosening a thumbscrew. This is the study for the three things that would make it a machine: moving the carriage up and down the arc, turning the rotor, and getting the phone to fire at the right moment and hand back the frames as one thing. It also takes the specimen mount seriously, because the tooth is the point and the spec gave it twenty millimeters of platform and no more thought.

1What the drives have to move

Every choice below follows from four numbers. The rotor side is the ring, the arc and its leg, the carriage, and the camera. There is one camera now — an iPhone in a spring clamp, 0.25 kg with the clamp. These numbers were written for a Sony α1 behind a 90 mm macro, 1.34 kg whose tripod socket sat 320 mm out, and that camera is no longer carried; everything below is remeasured for the phone, on the shortened carriage it allows.

0.76 kgrotor side (ring 295 g at 30% infill, arc 80, carriage 138, camera 250) — it was 1.8 kg
0.018 kg·m²its inertia about the rotor axis, from each body's own tensor moved to the axis — it was 0.10
3.7 Npull down the arc on the carriage at the 15° ring, 0.39 kg × g × cos 15° — it was 15 N
0.05 N·mto turn the rotor 10° in half a second; there is no detent to ride now

The same two consequences, with more margin. The rotor drive is easy: any small stepper through a modest reduction has forty times the torque needed, so the choice is about backlash, slip and holding, not power. The elevation drive is still about holding rather than moving — whatever carries the carriage must hold 3.7 N indefinitely with the power off, or it walks to the bottom of the arc between rings — but 3.7 N is a quarter of what the α1 asked, which widens what will do.

The ballast went with the α1. There were two stacks of washers, one in the rotor and one under the camera platform. The carriage's counterweight balanced a 1.34 kg body sitting 116 mm past the thumbscrew — 1.76 N·m, which the 16 mm tongue took as a 110 N couple; the phone, on a platform no longer stretched to reach an α1's socket, sits 27 mm past it and asks 6.5 N. The rotor's was never a counterweight at all: the rotor side's centre of mass is 114 mm off the axis at the lowest ring, 82 g·m of imbalance, and a Ø26 × 6 pocket holds about 25 g of washers — 2.7 g·m, three percent of it. Balancing it honestly would take 770 g of steel in a pocket 183 mm deep. The rotor does lift off one of its three bearings by 1.6 N at the lowest ring, and what holds it is the race flanks, which are drawn for axial hold.

2Changing elevation

The gravity idea, dropping a notch per ring from the top, fails on the geometry before it fails on anything else. The pull down the arc is g·cos θ and the friction holding a loose carriage is µ·g·sin θ. PETG on PETG is about µ = 0.4, so the carriage will not start sliding until θ is below 68°: parked at the 75° ring, it sits. At 35° the pull is nine times the friction, and even 0.39 kg arriving at the 15° stop from 20° up the arc is an impact and then a vibration. Gravity can seat a pawl; it cannot be the drive.

A · Index notches and a one-way pawl manual, do first

Four notches in the arc's outer flange at 75°, 55°, 35° and 15°, ramped on the uphill side and square on the downhill side. A sprung pawl on the saddle with a thumb lever. Lift the lever, ease the carriage down with the other hand, and the pawl drops onto the next square flank; the thumbscrew clamps for the ring. Working from the top down is right for this: the notch geometry means the carriage can only ever pass a notch going up under the lever and stops going down without it, so a slip costs one ring, not the whole arc.

  • Adds one printed pawl (PETG leaf spring, 1.5 mm, as the detent pawl was) and two M3 inserts to the saddle. The arc's fiducials become notches.
  • Repeatable to the notch: the pawl's flank sets θ to about ±0.2°, better than reading a tick.
  • No holding problem: the notch takes the load, the thumbscrew takes the vibration.

B · GT2 belt in the outboard channel, motor at the foot motorized

The arc's I-section has two channels between its flanges. The inboard one carries the saddle's tongue; the outboard one is empty. A 6 mm GT2 belt runs in it the length of the slot, around an idler at each end of the quadrant, and clamps to the outboard shoe. The motor sits on the arc's leg, at the foot, where its 200 g is 60 mm from the rotor axis instead of 170, and where it never has to be lifted. The flanges keep the belt captive; a GT2 belt bends happily around a 155 mm radius.

  • Holding: a 26 N·cm NEMA 17 on a 16-tooth GT2 pulley (5.1 mm pitch radius) holds 51 N, fourteen times the 3.7 N load, energized. Unpowered it holds nothing, so the notches from option A stay, or the firmware keeps the elevation motor energized at reduced current between rings (TMC2209 hold current at 30% is 8 N·cm, still 16 N, four times over).
  • Resolution: 3,200 microsteps per 32 mm of belt is 0.01 mm, or 0.004° at r = 155. Meaningless precision; what matters is repeatability, and a belt repeats to a tooth.
  • Belt length: the slot spans 4° to 86°, 222 mm of arc at r = 155, so the loop is about 480 mm plus the idlers. One meter of open belt covers it with a splice clamp printed into the shoe.
  • Cost to the print: two idler posts on the arc (M4 axles, the same 608-style top-hat sleeves or GT2 idlers with their own bearings), a motor mount on the leg, a belt clamp on the shoe. The arc still fits its plate.

C · Curved rack on the flange, pinion on the saddle

Module-1 teeth printed along the arc's outer face, a pinion on a knob or a stepper on the saddle. It is the most obviously "geared" answer and the worst of the three: the motor rides the carriage, adding its whole mass at r = 170 to every turn of the rotor and to the belt's job if both were built, and a printed rack on a 170 mm radius has to be sliced tooth by tooth without a seam. With a knob instead of a motor it beats nothing that option A does not do with fewer parts.

Build A now and B when a motor is on the table. They compose: the notches remain the unpowered hold and the belt does the moving, provided the rings run from the bottom up. Going up, the ratchet is free: the belt lifts the carriage past a notch, eases back, and the pawl takes the load. Going down needs the pawl lifted, so only the return to the bottom is done by hand. The carriage's thumbscrew stays: a phone's exposure is short, but a belt that can be back-driven is still a belt, and the screw is what makes a ring repeatable.

3Turning the rotor

The ring's outside is taken: the bearing race is in the middle of its 18 mm edge, and the 2.3 mm lips above and below it are too thin to carry teeth. The inside is free. The bore is 180 mm, and nothing is in it but the pedestal at the center.

A · Geneva drive on the bore built, version 3.0.0

A driver turns continuously; once per turn its pin enters a slot in a lip on the ring's bore, carries the ring one slot round, and leaves. For the rest of the turn a lock on the driver sits in a seat cut into the lip, and the ring cannot move either way, powered or not. One turn of the driver is one frame. The stop is set by the geometry, not by whatever turns the driver, and the ring starts and ends every step at rest. This replaced the 36 detent dimples and the leaf pawl, which held the ring lightly and had their own idea of where it should stop: a second decider beside any motor.

  • Where it is. A 5 mm lip on the bore, flush with the ring's underside so the ring still prints as it stands, with 36 slots and 36 lock seats. The driver stands inside the bore at r = 73, in a blind bushing on the base, and drops in from above through a lock seat after the ring is on. Its knob is reached from above with everything assembled; nothing hangs under the base.
  • Why r = 73. It is the largest radius at which the knob still passes inside the arc's bar, whose inner corner sweeps r = 82.8 three millimeters above the ring at the bottom of the dial.
  • Internal, not external. The pin works from inside the slot circle, so the slots open toward the axis and the lip stays joined to the ring's wall. The pin drives for 190° of each driver turn and the lock holds for 170°.
  • The lock is cut by the lip. It is a Ø6 disc less everything the lip sweeps through while the pin drives, taken in the driver's frame, so it follows any change to the slots. What is left is a crescent about 2.4 mm deep.
  • Measured, not assumed. The generator turns the driver through a whole revolution with the ring following the Geneva law and finds no contact; then nudges the ring 0.4° either way at a stop, where it must hit the lock, and mid-slot, where it must hit the pin. Both are gates, and the tests prove each instrument sees a jig made wrong in the one way it checks.
  • Play. 0.2 mm each side of a Ø3 steel pin in its slot and round the lock in its seat, both the shop's running fit because both move every frame: about 0.16° at the ring, 0.4 mm at the phone, against 0.25° for the printed gear below. The pin is pressed into a hole drawn Ø3.10, the shop's press; drawn at Ø2.9 it was an interference that would have split the crank.
  • Assembled, not assumed. The assembly page walks the 25 steps the generator checks: every piece along its path against everything already fitted, the bill of materials as the sum of the steps, every PETG fit measured and graded. That check is what found the ring could not be fitted at all with 3 mm race flanks and one eccentric, so the flanks are 1.4 mm and all three sleeves are eccentric.
  • What it asks of the protocol. Every step is a whole number of stops, so the rings go 10°, 10°, 20°, 20° rather than the spec's 10°, 12°, 15°, 20°. Still 108 frames: denser at 35°, coarser at 55°, where 20° spans what 11.5° does on the horizon.
  • The motor, later. A small DC gearmotor (an N20 at about 60 rpm) takes the knob's place and runs until a Hall sensor sees a magnet on the driver, then brakes. Where it actually stops does not matter as long as it is anywhere in the locked 170°. The mount for it is not drawn yet.

B · Internal ring gear in the bore, pinion on the base the stepper alternative

What this section recommended before the Geneva. It is kept because it is the route if continuous azimuth is ever wanted. Two changes it needs if it is built: the pinion sprung into the mesh, because nothing presses a vertical-axis gear to one side of its backlash, and no detent beside the stepper, because two things deciding where the ring stops will fight.

Module-1 teeth cut into the bore make a 178-tooth internal gear (pitch diameter 178 mm). A 16-tooth pinion on a NEMA 17 standing on the base at r = 81 meshes with it, hidden inside the ring, turning the same direction as the ring at 11.1:1. The teeth are 18 mm tall, printed vertically, which is the best orientation a printed gear can have.

  • Torque at the ring: 26 N·cm × 11.1 = 2.9 N·m, far more than turning needs. With the motor off the ring turns freely by hand; nothing indexes it.
  • Resolution: 200 steps × 16 microsteps × 11.1 = 35,500 per turn, 0.010°. Backlash in a printed module-1 mesh is 0.2 to 0.3° at the ring, and it only matters if you approach a frame from alternating directions. The firmware always approaches from the same side.
  • Homing: a nub on the ring's underside and a microswitch on the base, or TMC2209 StallGuard against a hard stop. Position is then dead-reckoned, which a gear allows and a friction roller does not.
  • Motor and cable on the stator: nothing on the rotor needs power except the elevation belt, whose motor is at the foot. One cable to the rotor side, with a loop of slack for one turn each way; the firmware never winds more than 360°.

C · A driven roller in place of one idler

Swap one 608 for a urethane roller of the same Ø22 on a motor, and the race the ring already has becomes the drive surface, at 10.7:1 for free. It is the smallest change to the part and the one I would not trust: the load on the ring is a 1.3 kg camera out on one side, so the contact force at the roller changes with azimuth, and a friction drive that slips by an unknown amount has to carry an encoder to know where it is. That is a magnetic ring encoder or an optical strip on a 242 mm ring, which is more work than the gear it was avoiding.

D · Worm on the rim

Self-locking, so the rotor holds against the camera's lopsided load with the power off, which is a real virtue. But the rim is taken by the race, a printed worm mesh is the least tolerant of any printed gear, and at 240:1 the ring turns 10° in about 1.3 s at a motor speed that is already near a stepper's torque cliff. If holding unpowered ever matters, a friction brake on the ring's top face is cheaper than a worm.

E · GT2 belt round the rim

Teeth printed on the rim at 2 mm pitch, 380 of them, are one-millimeter features at the edge of what a 0.4 mm nozzle draws cleanly, and again the rim is where the race lives. Ruled out by the race more than by the teeth.

The Geneva, built. It uses the one free surface, works by hand today and by motor later on the same driver, and makes the stops a property of the part. The detents and the pawl are gone.

Firmware. The ESP32 runs the same state machine the animation does, verbatim: SET_ELEVATION, ROTATE_AZIMUTH, SETTLE, CAPTURE, INCREMENT. It exposes one BLE service with three characteristics: protocol (the ring table as JSON, written by the app), command (home, start, pause, abort, go to stop n / θ), and status (state, ring, frame, φ, θ, notified on every change). CAPTURE is a handshake, not a timer: the jig announces it is settled, the phone takes the frame and acknowledges, and only then does the jig increment. Settling is measured rather than assumed: the app reads the phone's gyroscope and reports settled when rotation rate has been under 0.5°/s for 150 ms, which is typically well under the spec's 200 ms. Arduino with AccelStepper and TMCStepper is enough; there is nothing here that needs a real-time OS.

6Holding the tooth

The mount has three jobs that pull against each other: hold a 130 mm fossil still to a tenth of a millimeter through 108 settles, touch as little of it as possible so that what it touches is small enough to fill from the other pass, and be invisible to the reconstruction, which means matte, black, and featureless. A fourth job appears the moment the tooth is bigger than the frame: the mount has to work in two orientations, because no ring on this jig ever sees the underside of the root.

What does not work, and why

  • A clear acrylic rod or plate. Refraction moves the features seen through it and reflections add features that are not there. Structure-from-motion reads both as geometry. Transparent supports are the classic photogrammetry mistake.
  • Hanging it on monofilament. A single line lets the tooth swing and twist for seconds after every rotor move; three lines in a tripod stop that but need a gallows over the origin that clears the arc at 90°, so a post 200 mm up from the base outside r = 180. It is buildable and the line is thin enough to mask, but the settle time defeats the point of a jig that indexes in a second.
  • Putty alone for pass two. A megalodon tip on a lump of putty is a fossil balanced on its weakest point.

The specimen head system recommended

The pedestal's 20 mm platform becomes a socket: a 1/4-20 brass insert in the top, the same part the receiver uses, and a family of heads that screw onto it. Every head is printed in matte black PETG and painted with Black 3.0 or Musou, which is what the spec asked for on the pedestal and which matters more here, ten millimeters from the enamel. The focal-height dial already absorbs a head's height.

Two passes, one reconstruction. With the head masked out of every frame, the two passes see the same textured surface and differ only in what is hidden. Run them through a single structure-from-motion job rather than reconstructing each and aligning afterwards: the enameloid band appears in both, the matcher registers them, and the merge happens where it should, in the bundle adjustment. This is why the app has to know which pass a frame belongs to and why the masks matter more than the lighting.

What it needs from the jig

  • The pedestal gains one insert. The platform's dimple becomes the insert bore.
  • The three-pin cradle wants a slightly wider platform under it, Ø30, so the pedestal's cone starts 5 mm lower. The 75° sightline still clears: the draft rule is r ≤ 15 − d·tan 15° from a Ø30 platform, and the cone is recut to it.
  • The focal-height dial goes to 90 mm to put the inverted tooth's centroid on the origin with the cone under it. That is the ring's pad at 90 mm, still on the plate.

7The app and the asset

The reason to write an app is not the shutter. It is that the phone knows things about each frame that nothing downstream can recover, and it can hand them over only if something asks for them at the moment of capture.

  • The intrinsics, per frame. iOS delivers the 3×3 camera matrix with each photo when isCameraIntrinsicMatrixDeliveryEnabled is set on the connection. It changes frame to frame because the stabilizer moves the lens, and focus breathing changes the focal length. A reconstruction that assumes one fixed camera absorbs that as error.
  • The locks. Focus at a fixed lens position (setFocusModeLocked), exposure at a fixed duration and ISO, white balance fixed, 48 MP with maxPhotoDimensions, ProRAW if the pipeline wants it. Every frame identical except for where the camera is.
  • Settle, measured. The gyroscope says when the rotor has stopped ringing, per frame, instead of a fixed 200 ms.
  • The pose prior. The jig's kinematics give each frame's camera position to a millimeter and its orientation to a fraction of a degree before any image is looked at. Written into the manifest, they seed structure-from-motion instead of leaving it to guess, and they catch a frame that was taken at the wrong index.
  • The mask. iOS 17's Vision foreground-instance request lifts the specimen off the tent background on the phone, so every frame ships with a mask. The matte black head and the white tent make that mask nearly free, and the masks are what let the two passes register as one object.

Two versions

v1 · manual jig

No electronics. The app drives the operator: it shows the next ring and azimuth, watches the gyroscope for the settle, fires, and reads the pose off the phone itself. Elevation is the gravity vector in the phone's frame, which is exact for a camera bolted to the arc. Azimuth is the gyroscope's integrated yaw, re-zeroed at each ring's first frame at the pad's home mark; drift over one 40 s ring is under a degree, and the manifest records it as a prior with that uncertainty, not as truth. ARKit's world tracking would give a full pose but takes over the camera and caps frames at 12 MP on most bodies, so it is the fallback, not the default.

v2 · motorized jig

The app connects to the ESP32, writes the protocol, and runs the handshake in section 4. The operator presses one button. Poses come from the jig's indices and are exact to the mechanism's repeatability.

One asset

A capture is a folder, and the folder is the unit FossilRecord ingests: one specimen, one session, however many passes. Nothing in it needs the app to read.

8The order to do it in

phase 1

Manual jig, complete. The Geneva drive on the ring, wound by its knob, and the sole plate (both built in 3.0.0); notches and pawl on the arc and saddle; the pedestal insert and three heads: putty disc, three-pin cradle, tip cone; the platform widened to Ø30 and the dial to 90 mm. App v1 with locks, intrinsics, gyro settle, IMU poses, masks, and the folder. Every frame the spec asked for, taken properly, with nothing bought but pins, putty, foam and paint.

phase 2

Motors. A gearmotor in the Geneva knob's place, with its Hall sensor; arc reprinted with idler posts and the motor mount on the leg; shoe with the belt clamp; rings run bottom-up. The parts table in section 4, about $85. Firmware with the BLE service; app v2.

phase 3

The pipeline. A prior-seeded structure-from-motion run on both passes at once, masks in, then the splat. FossilRecord takes the folder as one asset and the model as its derivative. This is where the two-pass merge is proven on the Meherrin tooth before any other specimen goes on the jig.

Everything in phase 1 changes the generator and is swept the way the rest of the jig is. Phase 2 changes three printed parts and adds a fourth. If you want to start, the notches and the specimen heads are an afternoon in the generator, and the app's v1 is the part that turns frames into an asset regardless of what the jig becomes.
